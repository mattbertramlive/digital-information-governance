# Digital Information Governance (DIG®)

**An open reference framework for keeping AI-influenced decisions defensible and auditable.**

> Digital Information Governance (DIG) is a discipline for keeping AI-influenced decisions defensible and auditable, ensuring a company's information is accurately represented, its decisions are traceable, and its AI use is provable to regulators, partners, and courts.

DIG is decision-centric. It is distinct from *information governance* (the records and data lifecycle: storage, retention, disposition) and from *AI governance* in its narrow, model-centric sense (bias, drift, explainability). DIG governs the decision the model influences, and whether that decision can be defended after the fact.

## The four pillars

1. **Information Provenance.** Where the information feeding a decision came from, and whether it can be trusted.
2. **Decision Traceability.** A record of what was decided, by what (human or AI), on what basis, and who is accountable.
3. **Representation Integrity.** Keeping the company accurately represented across AI systems, search engines, and data environments.
4. **Audit Readiness.** Being able to prove, on demand, that AI-influenced decisions met their obligations.

## The DIG Maturity Model

A five-level scale measuring how defensible an organization's AI-influenced decisions are.

| Level | Name | In short |
|-------|------|----------|
| 1 | Ad hoc | No durable record. Decisions cannot be reconstructed. |
| 2 | Aware | Partial logging. Defensibility depends on the individual. |
| 3 | Defined | Four pillars are standard practice. Most decisions reconstructable on request. The first level a regulator would call governed. |
| 4 | Managed | Controls tested, audit-ready on demand, coverage measured. |
| 5 | Defensible by default | Decision integrity captured at decision time. Audit is continuous. |

## How DIG relates to the regulations

DIG is the decision-level layer that makes the major AI governance regimes operational: the [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework), the [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj) (Articles 11, 12, 14), [ISO/IEC 42001:2023](https://www.iso.org/standard/81230.html), and the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act (TRAIGA). Most of their obligations overlap, and all of them ultimately ask the same question: can you produce the record that shows an AI-influenced decision met its obligations?
